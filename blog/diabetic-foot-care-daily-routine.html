Diabetic Foot Care in the Treasure Valley: A Daily Routine That Prevents Complications

By Dr. Clark Johnson, DPM · September 1, 2026 · 8 min read · Diabetic Care

A small blister from a new pair of shoes. A cracked heel from our dry Treasure Valley summers. A callus that's been there for months. For most people, these are minor annoyances. For someone with diabetes, any one of them can become the starting point for a serious wound — and that's a risk worth taking seriously every single day.

The encouraging news is that the vast majority of diabetic foot complications are preventable. Research published through the NIH estimates that diabetic foot ulcers affect between 19% and 34% of individuals with diabetes at some point in their lifetime, and roughly 80% of non-traumatic lower-limb amputations are linked to diabetes and its effects on circulation and nerve function. Those are sobering numbers — but they also mean that a consistent daily routine, good footwear, and regular podiatry visits can shift the odds dramatically in your favor.

Why diabetes changes everything below the ankle

Two complications of poorly controlled diabetes hit the feet especially hard:

  • Peripheral neuropathy — nerve damage that reduces or eliminates the ability to feel pain, heat, or pressure in the feet. When neuropathy is present, an injury you'd normally notice immediately can go undetected for days.
  • Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) — narrowing of the blood vessels that supply the legs and feet, which slows wound healing and reduces the body's ability to fight infection.

Together, these two conditions create a dangerous situation: you may not feel a wound, and your body may struggle to heal it once it forms. That's the core reason diabetic foot care involves much more than just keeping feet clean — it's an active daily practice of inspection and protection. If you also experience numbness or tingling in your feet, that symptom may reflect neuropathy and is worth discussing with your care team.

Your daily diabetic foot care routine

The following steps are based on widely endorsed guidelines and represent the kind of routine we recommend to our patients at Treasure Valley Foot & Ankle. Think of it as five to ten minutes at the end of each day — a small investment that can prevent weeks or months of treatment.

1. Wash feet gently with mild soap and lukewarm water

Use your wrist or elbow — not your foot — to test the water temperature. Neuropathy can prevent you from feeling water that's actually scalding. Wash gently, rinse thoroughly, and keep soaks short (five minutes is plenty). Long soaks can soften and macerate skin, making it more vulnerable.

2. Dry thoroughly, especially between the toes

Moisture trapped between the toes is one of the most common entry points for fungal and bacterial infections. Pat the skin dry rather than rubbing, and pay extra attention to the spaces between each toe.

3. Moisturize — but not between the toes

Our Treasure Valley climate is notoriously dry, and diabetic skin tends to crack and fissure more easily than healthy skin. Apply a fragrance-free moisturizer to the tops and soles of your feet daily, but skip the area between the toes where moisture accumulates.

4. Inspect every surface of both feet

Use good lighting and a mirror if needed to see the bottoms of your feet. Look for:

  • Cuts, cracks, or blisters — even tiny ones
  • Redness, swelling, or warmth in any area
  • Changes in skin color or temperature
  • Calluses or corns that are growing or becoming painful
  • Any new sore or spot that was not there yesterday

If bending is difficult, ask a family member to check, or use a mirror propped on the floor. The goal is zero unexamined surfaces.

5. Trim nails straight across, not too short

Cut nails straight across, file sharp edges gently, and never cut into the corners. Cutting too short or rounding into the corners can create ingrown nails, which can quickly become infected in someone with diabetes. If your nails are thick, difficult to cut, or if vision or flexibility is limited, let a podiatrist handle nail care at each visit.

6. Never walk barefoot — indoors or out

This is one of the most important rules, and one of the most frequently overlooked. A step on a tack, a piece of gravel tracked in from the garden, even a hot concrete patio on a July afternoon near the Boise foothills — any of these can cause injury that goes unfelt. Wear well-fitting socks and shoes or diabetic slippers at all times, even around the house.

Treasure Valley summers and diabetic feet

Our summers are long, dry, and hot — which creates some specific hazards for people with diabetes. Hot pavement and sand can burn feet before neuropathy allows you to feel it. Sandals and flip-flops offer almost no protection and can cause blisters along the straps. Dehydration from the heat can also worsen circulation. Stick to closed-toe shoes with moisture-wicking socks during summer activities, stay hydrated, and check your feet every evening after any outdoor time.

Footwear: your most important protective tool

The right shoes do more protective work than almost any other aspect of diabetic foot care. Look for:

  • A wide, deep toe box that doesn't compress the toes
  • Seamless or smooth interiors to avoid pressure points
  • Firm, supportive soles with enough cushioning to protect against impact
  • Secure closures (laces or adjustable straps) rather than slip-ons
  • Breathable materials, especially important in warm weather

Always check the inside of a shoe before putting it on — a pebble or stitching that has come loose can cause an injury you won't feel. If standard shoes don't fit well due to foot structure changes, bunions, or swelling, ask about custom orthotics or therapeutic footwear. These are often covered by Medicare for qualifying patients with diabetes.

When to see a doctor — and when it's an emergency

If you have diabetes, the bar for seeking care is lower than for people without it. These situations call for a podiatry visit within 24–48 hours:

  • Any new wound, sore, or ulcer that has not improved in a day or two
  • A blister or cut that shows signs of infection (redness, warmth, drainage)
  • A callus that has developed a black or dark spot at its center — this can signal a wound forming beneath the surface
  • A toenail that appears infected (thickened, discolored, painful, or draining)
  • Any new numbness, burning, or tingling — this may signal worsening neuropathy
  • Swelling in one foot that is noticeably greater than the other

Go to the emergency room if you notice these signs

A wound with spreading redness, red streaks moving up the leg, significant swelling, or discharge combined with fever or chills can signal a serious infection that threatens the limb. This is a medical emergency — do not wait for a regular appointment. Also seek emergency care for any wound that exposes bone or tendon, or any sudden dramatic change in foot color (pale, blue, or dark). If you experience these symptoms, go to an emergency room immediately.

Between emergencies and routine visits, remember that any foot or ankle injury — even one that might seem minor — warrants prompt evaluation when diabetes is in the picture. A sprain, a stubbed toe, or a blister that would heal uneventfully in someone without diabetes may need professional attention in someone who does.

How a podiatrist fits into your diabetes care team

Most people with diabetes see a primary care physician or endocrinologist for blood sugar management, and that's essential. A podiatrist is the specialist who focuses specifically on the foot and ankle — performing comprehensive annual exams that assess circulation, nerve function, skin integrity, nail health, and foot structure. Think of it as a dedicated annual check-up for the part of your body most at risk from diabetes.

Beyond annual exams, our diabetic foot care services include safe nail and callus management for patients who can't safely do it themselves, wound care for early ulcers before they escalate, custom orthotics for patients with structural changes or pressure points, and coordination with your broader care team when more complex issues arise. When surgical intervention ever becomes necessary for complications like a Charcot foot deformity or an infected wound that hasn't healed, our foot and ankle surgery capabilities mean you can get the full spectrum of care without being referred out.

Frequently asked questions

How often should a person with diabetes inspect their feet?

Every single day — ideally at the same time each evening. Diabetic neuropathy can mask pain, so a cut, blister, or pressure sore you can't feel can progress quickly. Daily inspection catches problems while they're still small.

Can I use a pumice stone or callus shaver on my own?

Generally, people with diabetes should not use sharp instruments or aggressive tools like callus shavers at home. Even small cuts from these tools can become serious infections. A podiatrist can safely trim calluses and nails during routine visits.

How often should I see a podiatrist if I have diabetes?

Most guidelines recommend at least one comprehensive foot exam per year for patients with diabetes who have no current foot problems. Those with neuropathy, poor circulation, a history of ulcers, or other risk factors may benefit from visits every one to three months. Your podiatrist can help you determine the right schedule.

The bottom line

Diabetes doesn't have to mean losing a foot or a toe. It does mean taking foot health seriously every day — washing, inspecting, moisturizing, protecting, and knowing when something needs professional attention. The ten minutes you spend on your feet tonight may be the most important health investment you make this week.
