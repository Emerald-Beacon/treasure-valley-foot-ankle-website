Heel Injections and Advanced Heel Pain Treatments, Explained

By Dr. Clark Johnson, DPM · September 8, 2026 · 7 min read · Heel & Arch

You've stretched every morning. You've swapped your flip-flops for supportive shoes. You've iced the heel after every walk on the Greenbelt. And still, that first step out of bed feels like stepping on a hot nail. When conservative care hits its limits, it's time to talk about injections — and the newer advanced options that are giving many Treasure Valley patients lasting relief without surgery.

Heel pain is one of the most common reasons people visit a podiatrist, driven largely by plantar fasciitis — irritation of the thick band of tissue that runs along the bottom of the foot. Research estimates it accounts for approximately one million provider visits per year in the United States alone, and roughly 10% of the general population will experience it at some point. For most patients, consistent stretching, supportive footwear, and physical therapy resolve the problem over time. But for those whose pain lingers for months despite doing everything right, targeted injections and advanced therapies can provide a meaningful reset.

Here is an honest, evidence-based look at every major option — what each one does, what the research actually shows, and how I think about choosing the right treatment for each patient at Treasure Valley Foot & Ankle.

Cortisone (corticosteroid) injections: the most-requested option

Cortisone shots are the most commonly used injection for heel pain, and for good reason: they work quickly. A corticosteroid is a powerful anti-inflammatory agent. When injected near the origin of the plantar fascia at the heel, it can dramatically reduce pain and swelling within days.

What the research says

Clinical studies consistently show that cortisone injections provide significant short-term pain relief. The catch is the word "short-term" — research places the typical duration of benefit at roughly four to twelve weeks. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis published in PMC confirmed that ultrasound-guided corticosteroid injections are superior to blind injections, particularly for reducing plantar fascia thickness and improving mechanical pain tolerance — so how the shot is delivered matters as much as the shot itself.

The limits and risks to know about

Cortisone is not a cure. It quiets the inflammation, but it doesn't address the underlying tightness, biomechanical factors, or tissue damage driving the problem. Repeated injections also carry a small but real risk of weakening the plantar fascia over time, which is why most podiatrists limit cortisone shots to two or three per year in the same site. If heel pain keeps coming back after each round of cortisone, it's a signal to move to a treatment that addresses the root cause.

Who is the best candidate?

  • Patients with acute or subacute plantar fasciitis who need fast pain relief to stay active or work comfortably.
  • Those who have tried stretching and footwear changes but need a bridge while longer-term rehab takes effect.
  • Patients who haven't had more than one or two prior cortisone injections at the same site.

Why ultrasound guidance matters at our office

A "blind" injection means the clinician places the needle by anatomical landmarks alone. Ultrasound-guided injection means we watch the needle tip in real time on a screen and confirm the medication goes exactly where it needs to go. A 2025 meta-analysis found that ultrasound guidance produces superior outcomes compared with blind technique — better pain relief and a measurable reduction in plantar fascia thickness. At Treasure Valley Foot & Ankle, we use ultrasound guidance for our heel injections to maximize accuracy and minimize the risk of placing medication in the wrong tissue.

PRP (platelet-rich plasma) injections: a longer-lasting alternative

Platelet-rich plasma therapy has been one of the most exciting developments in podiatric medicine over the last decade. It's become a popular question in our Meridian office, especially among active Treasure Valley patients who don't want to keep cycling through cortisone shots.

How PRP works

PRP is made from your own blood. A small sample is drawn at the office, spun in a centrifuge to concentrate the platelets and growth factors, and then injected back into the damaged tissue. Instead of suppressing inflammation the way cortisone does, PRP works by stimulating the tissue's own repair process — it can encourage the body to lay down new collagen and heal the microscopic tears in the fascia that keep driving pain.

What the evidence shows

Multiple randomized controlled trials have compared PRP directly to corticosteroid injections for plantar fasciitis. The pattern in the research is consistent: cortisone often wins in the first few weeks, but by the six-month mark, PRP tends to produce superior pain relief and better functional outcomes. A prospective study published in PMC reported a success rate of approximately 80% for autologous PRP in chronic plantar fasciitis cases, with patients showing significant improvement in both pain scores and plantar fascia thickness on ultrasound. PRP also has a favorable safety profile because the material comes from your own body.

Who is the best candidate for PRP?

  • Patients with chronic plantar fasciitis (symptoms lasting more than three to six months) who have not responded adequately to cortisone or conservative care.
  • Those who want a treatment aimed at actual tissue healing rather than temporary inflammation control.
  • Patients willing to wait four to six weeks for the full benefit to develop.
  • People who are not good candidates for repeated cortisone shots due to prior injections at the same site.

One practical note: most insurance plans do not cover PRP for plantar fasciitis, classifying it as investigational. Ask our team about current pricing before scheduling, and we will be upfront about what to expect.

Extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT): non-injection advanced care

Shockwave therapy is not an injection at all — it uses focused acoustic energy delivered through the skin to stimulate healing in the plantar fascia. It has earned strong interest as a treatment for chronic, treatment-resistant heel pain, and it is backed by a growing body of comparative research. A recent prospective randomized controlled trial published in PMC compared ESWT with corticosteroid injection and radiofrequency ablation for chronic plantar fasciitis and found each to be effective, with ESWT showing durable improvement for patients who had not responded to prior injections.

Shockwave therapy typically requires a series of sessions, and results build gradually over six to twelve weeks. It can be a compelling option for patients who want to avoid injections entirely, or for those who have exhausted injection-based options. Ask us whether it may be a fit for your situation.

So which treatment is right for you?

There is no universal answer — it depends on how long you've had pain, how many prior treatments you've tried, your activity goals, and your health history. Here's how I generally approach it in clinic:

  • For a patient with pain lasting less than three months who hasn't tried an injection: a cortisone shot combined with a structured stretching and rehab program is often the right first step.
  • For a patient who has already had one or two cortisone shots and is still struggling after several months: PRP becomes a very attractive next option, given its stronger long-term data.
  • For a patient who has been battling chronic heel pain for over a year and has tried multiple treatments: shockwave therapy, PRP, or a combination approach may offer the best path forward.
  • In all cases, injections and advanced therapies work best when paired with addressing the root causes — tight calf muscles, biomechanical factors, activity levels, and footwear. An injection that quiets the pain is an opportunity to do the rehab work, not a reason to skip it.

A note for active Treasure Valley patients

Whether you're training for a fall race on the Greenbelt, getting in your last foothills hikes before Bogus Basin opens, or simply want to walk the farmers market without wincing, you don't have to accept "just rest it" as the only answer. Advanced heel pain treatments exist precisely to get active people back to doing what they love — faster and with less risk of surgery. If your heel pain has stuck around longer than a couple of months, it's worth a conversation about whether one of these options could help.

When to see a doctor — and when heel pain is urgent

Most heel pain is not an emergency, but a few situations call for prompt or immediate attention:

  • Sharp, sudden heel pain after a misstep or impact: this may indicate a stress fracture or a plantar fascia rupture, not just ordinary fasciitis. Get evaluated the same day.
  • Redness, warmth, and swelling spreading beyond the heel, especially with fever or chills: these can signal an infection and need urgent care.
  • Numbness or tingling in the heel or arch: nerve involvement (such as tarsal tunnel syndrome) changes the diagnosis and the treatment plan.
  • Heel pain in a person with diabetes or poor circulation: even seemingly minor foot pain warrants a prompt podiatry visit, since healing can be slower and complications more serious.
  • Pain that is worsening despite several weeks of rest and conservative care: don't wait it out indefinitely — a proper evaluation may reveal a more specific cause that responds to targeted treatment.

Frequently asked questions

How many cortisone shots can I get for heel pain?

Most podiatrists limit cortisone injections to two or three per year in the same area. Repeated high-dose steroid injections can weaken the plantar fascia over time, which carries a small risk of fascia rupture. If heel pain persists after a couple of cortisone shots, it is worth exploring alternatives like PRP or shockwave therapy.

Does a cortisone shot for heel pain hurt?

Heel injections can be uncomfortable because the heel pad is dense, but most patients tolerate them well. The discomfort is typically brief. Ultrasound guidance allows for more precise placement, which can reduce unnecessary discomfort compared to a blind injection.

Is PRP covered by insurance for heel pain?

In most cases, PRP injections are not covered by standard insurance plans for plantar fasciitis because many insurers still classify them as investigational. Our office can discuss current pricing and whether your specific plan offers any coverage before you decide.

How long does it take for heel pain to resolve after an injection?

Cortisone often brings noticeable relief within days to two weeks but may last only four to twelve weeks. PRP can take four to six weeks to show full benefit but tends to last considerably longer in research studies. Shockwave therapy results typically build over six to twelve weeks after the treatment series. All three options work best when combined with stretching and addressing the underlying cause of your pain.

The bottom line

Stubborn heel pain has more solutions today than ever before. A cortisone shot can provide fast, meaningful relief for the right patient. PRP can offer a more durable outcome when longer-lasting results are the goal. Shockwave therapy can reach patients who haven't responded to injections at all. The key is a proper diagnosis and a treatment plan matched to your specific situation — not a one-size-fits-all approach.
