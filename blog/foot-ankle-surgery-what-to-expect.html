Foot or Ankle Surgery: What to Expect From Consultation to Recovery

By Dr. Clark Johnson, DPM · August 25, 2026 · 8 min read · Surgery

For most of my patients, the words "you may need surgery" bring on a wave of anxiety — not because the procedure itself is terrifying, but because the unknown is. What will they do? How long will I be off my feet? When can I hike the foothills again? The good news is that foot and ankle surgery is one of the most predictable areas of orthopedic care, and knowing what each phase looks like takes most of the fear out of the equation.

This guide walks you through the entire journey from a surgical consultation at Treasure Valley Foot & Ankle to the day you're back on your feet — literally. Whether you're weighing bunion surgery, exploring options for ankle arthritis, or simply want to understand what our foot and ankle surgery process looks like before committing, this is written for you.

Do you actually need surgery? The conservative-first approach

Responsible foot and ankle care almost always begins with non-surgical options. Surgery is the right choice when conservative treatments have been genuinely tried and haven't given you enough relief to maintain the quality of life you want.

Common non-surgical steps we work through first include:

  • Activity modification — reducing or adjusting the activities that aggravate the problem.
  • Custom orthotics or supportive footwear — offloading pressure and improving mechanics.
  • Physical therapy — building strength, flexibility, and stability around the affected joint.
  • Bracing or casting — immobilizing the area to let inflammation settle.
  • Injections — corticosteroid or other targeted injections for pain and swelling management.

When those steps don't provide lasting relief — or when the structural problem (like a severely displaced bunion or end-stage joint destruction) is unlikely to improve without correction — surgery becomes the most logical path forward.

Your consultation: what to expect in the room

A surgical consultation isn't a commitment — it's a conversation. Here's what typically happens:

  • History and exam: We review your symptoms, what you've already tried, and examine the foot or ankle carefully.
  • Imaging: X-rays are standard; MRI or CT may be ordered depending on the suspected problem.
  • Discussion of options: We explain what's happening structurally, walk through surgical versus non-surgical choices, and discuss the procedure we'd recommend along with realistic outcomes.
  • Recovery planning: We cover what you'll need at home, how long you'll be non-weight-bearing, and what work and activity restrictions to expect.
  • Your questions: No question is too basic. Bring a list if it helps — a friend or family member is always welcome to join you.

Questions worth asking at your consultation

What procedure do you recommend, and are there alternatives? What type of anesthesia will be used? How long will I be completely off my feet? When can I drive, return to desk work, and return to hiking or the gym? Will I need physical therapy, and for how long? What are the most likely complications, and how common are they?

Common foot and ankle surgeries: a side-by-side look

Different procedures carry very different recovery expectations. Here's a general overview of the procedures we perform most often in Meridian — your individual plan will be tailored to your anatomy, activity goals, and health history.

Procedure Typical setting Non-weight-bearing Full recovery estimate
Bunion correction (bunionectomy) Same-day outpatient 2–6 weeks depending on technique Up to 6 months for full function
Ankle arthroscopy Same-day outpatient Days to a few weeks A few weeks to a few months
Ankle fusion (arthrodesis) Outpatient or short stay 6–8 weeks non-weight-bearing 4–6 months
Total ankle replacement Outpatient or short stay 4–6 weeks 6–12 months
Tendon repair / reconstruction Same-day outpatient 4–8 weeks in cast or boot 4–6 months

Research published in Foot & Ankle International has documented a well-recognized three-month postoperative adaptation phase in foot and ankle surgery — a window when bone, tendon, and soft tissue are consolidating and functional gains often feel slow. This is normal, expected, and not a sign anything went wrong.

Surgery day: what actually happens

The night before your procedure you'll typically fast from midnight onward. On surgery day:

  • You'll check in at the surgical center, usually an hour or more before your scheduled time.
  • A nurse reviews your medications, allergies, and medical history.
  • An IV is placed and your anesthesiologist discusses the anesthetic plan — many foot and ankle procedures use a regional nerve block combined with light sedation, which means you're comfortable, the foot is numb for hours after surgery, and you avoid the grogginess of a general anesthetic.
  • The procedure itself often takes 45 minutes to 2 hours depending on complexity.
  • You recover in a post-op area until the nursing staff confirms you're stable, then go home with a responsible adult driver. You cannot drive yourself home from surgery.

Week-by-week recovery: what the milestones look like

Recovery is not linear — there are good days and frustrating plateaus — but the general arc is predictable. Here's a rough guide for a moderately complex procedure like bunion correction or tendon repair:

  • Week 1–2: Elevation, rest, ice, and wound care are the priorities. Swelling and discomfort are expected and managed with the pain plan from your surgeon. Keep the foot elevated above heart level as much as possible.
  • Weeks 3–6: Your surgeon will re-examine the wound and take X-rays to confirm healing. You may transition from a splint to a surgical boot and begin partial weight-bearing if bone healing is on track.
  • Weeks 6–12: Physical therapy often begins here — range-of-motion exercises, gentle strengthening, and gait training. Swelling at the end of the day is still normal.
  • Months 3–6: Most patients return to regular shoes and light activity. The three-month mark is frequently when people feel they've turned a corner, though full strength and endurance take longer.
  • Months 6–12: For more complex procedures like ankle replacement, final functional gains are still occurring. Studies show that more than half of total ankle replacement patients reach normal function scores by six months, with continued improvement possible up to a year.

Recovery in a Treasure Valley summer (or any season)

Timing surgery strategically can make recovery smoother. Our hot, dry summers are great for the outdoor activities you're working toward, but the first weeks post-op call for a cool, quiet setup at home. Many patients schedule surgery in late summer so they're walking well by fall — just in time for the Greenbelt and foothills trails. If you have a specific goal (the City of Trees Marathon, a ski season at Bogus Basin), tell us at your consultation. Timing matters and we'll factor it in.

Tips that genuinely speed up recovery

What you do between visits has a big impact on how smoothly recovery goes:

  • Elevate religiously — the single most effective way to control swelling, especially in the first two weeks.
  • Protect the incision — keep it dry and covered until cleared by your surgeon. Infection is a real risk and the most common avoidable complication.
  • Don't rush weight-bearing — bones heal on their own schedule. Loading a repair too early can set healing back by weeks.
  • Attend every follow-up — X-rays at these visits confirm that bone is healing as expected before we advance your activity.
  • Be honest about pain — if something feels wrong or pain is increasing rather than slowly improving after the first week, call us. Catching a problem early is always better.
  • Stay active within restrictions — upper-body exercise, chair exercises, and staying mentally engaged all support recovery.

Medical disclaimer and when to seek urgent care after surgery

The information in this article is for general education and is not a substitute for personalized medical advice from your surgeon. Every patient and every procedure is different — the timelines and milestones above are general guides, not promises. Always follow the specific instructions your surgical team gives you.

After foot or ankle surgery, contact your surgeon's office promptly if you notice:

  • Increasing pain, swelling, or warmth at the wound that is getting worse, not better, after the first week
  • Drainage, odor, or opening of the incision
  • Fever above 101°F (38.3°C)
  • Redness spreading away from the incision site
  • New numbness or tingling not explained by the nerve block wearing off

Go to an emergency room immediately if you develop sudden severe calf pain or swelling, shortness of breath, or chest pain — these can be signs of a deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or pulmonary embolism, serious complications that require immediate evaluation.

Frequently asked questions

How long does foot or ankle surgery recovery take?

It depends entirely on the procedure. Ankle arthroscopy may allow return to light activity in weeks. Bunion surgery often takes up to six months for full function. Total ankle replacement can take six to twelve months. Your surgeon will give you a personalized timeline at your consultation.

Will I need to stay in the hospital?

Most foot and ankle surgeries are same-day outpatient procedures — you go home the same day. More complex reconstructions may involve a short hospital stay. Your team will confirm the plan well before your procedure date.

When is surgery actually necessary?

Surgery is typically recommended after conservative treatments — therapy, orthotics, bracing, and injections — have been tried for an appropriate time without enough relief, or when a structural problem is unlikely to improve without correction. The decision is always individualized.

The bottom line

Foot and ankle surgery is not something to fear — it's something to understand. The patients who do best are the ones who come into it with clear expectations, a solid support plan for the first few weeks at home, and the patience to follow the recovery protocol. The reward — walking without pain, getting back on the Greenbelt, or finally keeping up with the grandkids — is absolutely worth it for the right patient.
